\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A three-model CGCNN ensemble predicts bulk and shear modulus for every
material in a 554{,}054-entry snapshot of Google DeepMind's GNoME discovery
database; the paper's own Slack-model physics turns those moduli into a
predicted lattice thermal conductivity \KL{}; and 16{,}299 candidates clear
the screening threshold of \KL{} $\leq 1$~W~m$^{-1}$~K$^{-1}$. That screen,
and the model behind it, are described in full in \texttt{docs/method.tex}.
This document is the follow-on: having a candidate list is not the same as
understanding it, so every one of those 33{,}323 scored candidates is here
tagged with its crystallographic space group (read directly from GNoME's own
structure metadata) and its constituent elements, then broken down two ways
--- across the full screen, and across the 11{,}612 oxide-containing
candidates specifically --- to ask what the low-\KL{} region of chemical
space actually looks like. Two findings stand out. First, low-\KL{}
candidates are not uniformly distributed across chemistry: halogens (Br, I,
Cl, F) are markedly \emph{enriched} among low-\KL{} hits (up to $1.9\times$
their background rate) while oxygen is markedly \emph{depleted}
($0.27\times$), consistent with soft, heavy-halide lattices having lower
sound velocities and Debye temperatures than tightly-bonded oxides. Second,
both the general screen and the oxide subset skew heavily toward
low-symmetry space groups (triclinic and monoclinic account for two-thirds
of all candidates), a pattern this document quantifies precisely rather than
leaves as an impression. Finally, we identify the single strongest candidate
in every crystal system and every space group, for both populations ---
ranked not by the raw point-estimate \KL{}, but by its 95th-percentile Monte
Carlo value, since ranking $>30$k candidates by the point estimate alone
turns out to be a textbook winner's-curse setup: the top point-estimate
oxide and the runner-up general candidate both carry $19$--$22\times$
p95/p05 uncertainty ratios, indistinguishable from noise rather than a
confident low-\KL{} prediction. Re-ranked this way,
\textbf{CrSi\textsubscript{3}Cl\textsubscript{16}} (triclinic, $P1$) holds
its \#1 spot in the general screen (p95 $=0.0094$~W~m$^{-1}$~K$^{-1}$,
$7.6\times$ ratio); the oxide leaderboard reorders, with
\textbf{COF\textsubscript{14}} (monoclinic, $P2_1$) taking the top spot at a
much tighter $2.3\times$ ratio. \textbf{That p95/p05 ranking itself turned
out to be overconfident}: checked against 1{,}648 real DFT-labelled test
crystals (Section~6), the ensemble's claimed 90\% interval actually covers
only 48--51\% of true values --- a validated, out-of-sample recalibration
widens every interval 4.3--4.5$\times$, after which COF\textsubscript{14}
still holds its \#1 oxide spot, but CrSi\textsubscript{3}Cl\textsubscript{16}
is displaced from the general-screen top spot by
\textbf{SbP\textsubscript{2}Cl\textsubscript{13}}. The ranking survives
better than any individual confidence claim does.
\end{abstract}

\section{Model and physics summary}

\subsection{The network: CGCNN}

Each crystal becomes a graph --- atoms as nodes, bonds as edges --- and the
network predicts \KV{} and \GV{} (Voigt--Reuss--Hill bulk and shear modulus)
from it directly, with no DFT calculation involved.

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Nodes.} Each atom is a 92-dimensional vector of tabulated
  element properties (group, period, electronegativity, radius, \ldots),
  looked up by atomic number, not learned.
\item \textbf{Edges.} Every atom's 12 nearest neighbours within 8~\AA{},
  respecting periodic boundary conditions, with the bond distance expanded
  onto 41 Gaussian basis functions rather than used as a single raw number.
\item \textbf{Convolution (gated message passing), three layers.} For atom
  $i$ with neighbours $j$ and bond feature $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$:
  \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}_i^{(t+1)} = \mathbf{v}_i^{(t)} + \sum_{j}
      \underbrace{\sigma(\mathbf{z}_{ij}^{(t)}\mathbf{W}_f + \mathbf{b}_f)}_{\text{gate}}
      \odot
      \underbrace{g(\mathbf{z}_{ij}^{(t)}\mathbf{W}_s + \mathbf{b}_s)}_{\text{message}},
    \qquad \mathbf{z}_{ij}^{(t)} = \mathbf{v}_i^{(t)} \oplus \mathbf{v}_j^{(t)} \oplus \mathbf{e}_{ij}
  \end{equation}
  with $\sigma$ a sigmoid (how much of a neighbour's message to admit) and
  $g$ a softplus (what that message says). Shared weights across every edge
  are what make this a \emph{convolution}; the residual term lets gradients
  reach every layer.
\item \textbf{Pooling.} The crystal-level vector is the \emph{mean} over its
  atoms (not the sum), so the prediction does not change if the unit cell is
  doubled --- essential for an intensive property like a modulus.
\item \textbf{Ensembling.} Three independently-initialised models per
  target, averaged in log space; their spread is a free per-crystal
  uncertainty estimate with no equivalent in a single pre-trained model.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The physics: Slack model}

Everything from $(K, G)$ to \KL{} is closed-form physics, unchanged from the
paper, driven by our own predicted moduli:
\begin{align}
  v_{\text{long}} &= \sqrt{(K + \tfrac{4}{3}G)/\rho}, \qquad
  v_{\text{trans}} = \sqrt{G/\rho} \\
  \gamma &= \frac{3(1+\nu)}{2(2-3\nu)}, \qquad
  \nu = \frac{(v_{\text{long}}/v_{\text{trans}})^2 - 2}{2(v_{\text{long}}/v_{\text{trans}})^2 - 2} \\
  \kappa_L &= \frac{G \cdot v_{\text{sound}} \cdot V^{1/3}}{N_{\text{atoms}} \cdot 300}\, e^{-\gamma}
  \qquad \text{(Eq.~2 of the PINK paper, } \delta=1\text{)}
\end{align}
where $\nu$ is Poisson's ratio and $\gamma$ the Gr\"uneisen parameter ---
\textbf{both derived entirely from the predicted $K$ and $G$}, the same
formula the paper's own released \texttt{pink\_predict.py} uses for real
screening (its Table~1 validation numbers use a different, literature-looked-up
$\gamma$ instead; see \texttt{docs/method.tex}, Section~7.4, for why that
distinction matters and is not a bug in this pipeline).

\keybox{Where the space-group data in this document comes from}{Every
Space Group / Space Group Number / Crystal System value below is read
directly from GNoME's own \texttt{stable\_materials\_summary.csv} --- it is
part of the structure metadata GNoME itself computed when the material was
generated, joined onto our scored candidates by \texttt{MaterialId}. No new
structure analysis was run for this document; 0.8--0.9\% of candidates have
no matching summary row (a small gap in the upstream file, not something
this project introduces) and are reported, not silently dropped.}

For every element appearing anywhere in the screen, we compute how many
candidates contain it and define an \emph{enrichment ratio}: the fraction of
low-\KL{} candidates containing that element, divided by its fraction of the

\KL{} more than average; $<1$ means the opposite.

\section{The strongest candidates}

``Best'' below means lowest \KL{} \emph{p95} --- the 95th-percentile Monte

\keybox{Why rank by p95, not the point estimate}{Ranking 33{,}323 candidates
by lowest point-estimate \KL{} and taking the minimum is a textbook
winner's-curse setup: a candidate needs either a genuinely low \KL{} or just
a wide, noisy ensemble disagreement to land at the top, and the two look
identical in a point estimate alone. Checked directly on this data: ranking
by the point estimate instead of p95, the top oxide (ReAsOF\textsubscript{10})

noisy ones. The ratio column below makes that confidence visible rather than
hiding it behind a single number.}

\section{Is the uncertainty interval actually calibrated?}

Section~5's entire premise --- that ranking by \KL{} p95 rather than the
point estimate protects against noisy predictions --- depends on one
assumption never yet tested: that the ensemble's claimed 90\% interval
(the gap between \texttt{Kappa\_cal\_p05} and \texttt{Kappa\_cal\_p95})
actually contains the true value about 90\% of the time.
\texttt{docs/method.tex} already flags this as ``weak by construction,''
since real experimental \KL{} exists for only 2 materials our ensemble
never trained on --- nowhere near enough to check. But the uncertainty
itself comes from disagreement on $K$ and $G$, not \KL{} directly, and the
matbench \emph{test} split --- 1{,}648 crystals with real DFT-computed $K$
and $G$, genuinely held out from training --- already has everything needed
to check that half, with no new data required.

\subsection{The check}

For each of the 1{,}648 test crystals, does the true $\log_{10}$ modulus
fall inside the ensemble's own claimed interval
($\text{pred} \pm z \cdot \sigma_{\text{ensemble}}$, $z=1.6449$ for a 90\%
normal interval — mathematically the same claim
\texttt{monte\_carlo\_kappa()}'s 2{,}000-draw sampling converges to, just
without sampling noise)? Repeated at every nominal level from 10\% to 90\%,
not just the one used for screening.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../results/cgcnn/calibration_check.png}
\caption{Reliability diagram: nominal coverage (claimed by the interval) vs.
empirical coverage (actual, on 1{,}648 held-out test crystals). Left: the
raw ensemble-spread interval used everywhere in this document up to this
point. Right: after the recalibration in Section~6.2, with the correction
factor fit on the (disjoint) validation split and checked on test.}
\end{figure}

\keybox{Badly overconfident, not roughly right}{At the 90\% level used
throughout this document, the ACTUAL coverage is \textbf{50.8\%} for $K$
and \textbf{47.9\%} for $G$ --- barely more than half of what the interval
claims. The cause: ensemble spread measures how much three
identically-trained models \emph{disagree with each other}, which is a
much smaller quantity than how far they collectively sit from the truth.
Checked directly: median ensemble spread is 0.018--0.028 $\log_{10}$, but
the actual test RMSE is 0.118 for both moduli --- the real error is
4--6$\times$ larger than ensemble disagreement alone suggests. Three
models sharing one architecture and one training set agree with each
other even when they share the same blind spots.}

\subsection{The fix, checked out-of-sample}

A scale factor derived from the \emph{validation} split (a different 1,648
crystals from test) and applied to test --- so the check is not circular
--- widens the interval to $K{:}~4.27\times$, $G{:}~4.54\times$ (on top of
the untouched $z=1.645$), and recovers \textbf{89.5\%} ($K$) and
\textbf{90.9\%} ($G$) actual coverage on test: right on target,
out-of-sample.

\subsection{What changes when this is applied to Section 5's own picks}

Nothing about the model changes --- $K_{\mathrm{VRH}}$, \GV{}, and the
point-estimate \KL{} are byte-for-byte identical. Only how wide the error
bars are reported changes. Re-scoring the same 14 crystal-system winners
from Table~6 with the validated factor:

\noindent
The fixed 2$\times$/5$\times$ tight/moderate/wide cutoffs (Section~3's
convention) were calibrated by eye against the \emph{old}, artificially
narrow interval; they do not carry over meaningfully once the interval is
corrected, where every single one of these 14 headline picks now lands
deep in ``wide'' territory. This is a known failure mode of small deep
ensembles: disagreement among a handful of identically-architected,
identically-trained members is a poor proxy for true predictive error,
because it captures variance from random initialisation but not the bias
every member shares. \textbf{The practical conclusion}: the \emph{ranking}
remains the most defensible thing to trust --- COF14 and SbP2Cl13 are still
the best \emph{available} picks after recalibration --- but no individual
candidate's uncertainty interval should be read as a genuine confidence
guarantee until a fundamentally better-calibrated uncertainty method --- a
larger ensemble, conformal prediction, or an approach that captures model
bias rather than only member disagreement --- replaces the current one.
This is now the single highest-leverage next step identified in this
document, ahead of finding more candidates.
